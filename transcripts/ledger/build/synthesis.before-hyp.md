# Synthesis: Werner, Krainer, Berletic, Dixon, Fitts, Norton, Henningsen, Martenson, Hudson, Crooke, Wilkerson, Diesen, Webb, Wolff

Twelfth pass, 2026-10-03. Built from 15 interview transcripts in this directory plus about 170 of the authors' own articles, book chapters and interviews (list at the end). Ben Norton was added in the third pass, K.J. Noh in the fourth (he became a cited reference in the twelfth), Patrick Henningsen in the fifth, Chris Martenson in the sixth, Michael Hudson in the seventh, Alastair Crooke and Lawrence Wilkerson in the eighth, Glenn Diesen and Whitney Webb in the ninth, in the tenth a companion document, hidden-layer.md, on Richard Dolan, Steven Greer, Richard Doty and John Lear. Their points of overlap with the others are kept here. Richard Wolff was added in this one. Noam Chomsky and K.J. Noh are cited as references and are not treated as authors.

## Method and limits

This document reports what the fourteen authors argue and compares them with each other. Four further authors (Dolan, Greer, Doty and Lear), whose subject is secret programs, withheld technology and a non-human presence, have their own document, hidden-layer.md; they appear here only where they meet the fourteen. It does not check their claims against official statistics or reference works. Claims are tested four ways:

1. Against each other: where do fourteen people reach the same conclusion, and where do they contradict?
2. Against the documents they cite themselves.
3. For internal consistency.
4. Against events that all of them describe, so their forecasts can be compared.

## Short version

- All fourteen say real power sits above elected government. Most of them locate it in finance: control of credit, collateral and the payment system. Henningsen adds old money, insurance and campaign donors, and puts the media at the centre of how consent is obtained. Martenson puts energy underneath finance: money and debt are claims on output, and output needs energy. Hudson gives the longest history: a class of creditors and rent-takers that has run states through their finances since the medieval church created international banking. Crooke puts the deepest layer in culture and meaning, not money. Wilkerson, the one author who served inside the policy machine and broke with it publicly, describes careerism, donors and hatred more than a plan. Diesen, a realist, needs no hidden ruler at all: a hegemon's behaviour is predictable from its position. Webb goes furthest the other way: "a handful of men with bottomless pockets who were willing to wait decades", working through intelligence, finance and technology.
- They disagree on who sits at the top, and on whether anyone is hidden there. Krainer describes a "monolithic and ruthless conspiracy". Dixon says there is no organised cabal. Norton and Wolff say the rulers are a class acting largely in the open; for Wolff it is the employer class as a whole, not finance in particular.
- The deepest unresolved question is the remedy. Krainer, Berletic, Norton, Henningsen, Hudson, Crooke, Wilkerson and Diesen want sovereign states that discipline finance and resist empire. Werner, Fitts, Dixon, Martenson and Webb want decentralisation and fear the digital state, though Webb distrusts the crypto exits too. Each camp's remedy is the other's main fear. Wolff offers a third remedy that is neither: democratise the enterprise itself, making employees their own employers in worker cooperatives, private and state-owned enterprises alike. Henningsen has a foot in both: he also opposes digital ID, central bank digital currency and global taxation. Thirteen of the fourteen admire something about China; Webb does not address it in what I read.
- On the one test thirteen of the fourteen faced, Trump and the 2026 war on Iran, Berletic, Norton and Henningsen expected no change from Trump and needed no revision. Krainer, Werner, Martenson and Wilkerson changed their reading of Trump afterwards, though Wilkerson had forecast the war itself. By mid-2026 seven of the thirteen read the war as aimed at least partly at China; Wolff reads it as a desperate attempt to reverse imperial decline, and as political theatre. Henningsen calls it "a war for Israel" that serves no US interest.
- Four more authors, Dolan, Greer, Doty and Lear, describe a layer the fourteen barely touch: programs funded off the books, technology kept from the public, and, in their account, a non-human presence that those programs have studied since the 1940s. Greer goes furthest: the entity running them has "more power than any single government", is part government program and part "privatized organized crime", and holds energy technology that would end the oil order. Fitts, one of the fourteen, already describes the money side, a "covert economy" fed by the black budget. They are compared in their own document, hidden-layer.md.
- Each has a piece the others lack. The section "What each could teach the others" sets these out.
- Their practical advice overlaps heavily, and most of it does not depend on settling who is at the top.

## What all fourteen agree on

- **Decisions are made above elected government.** Werner: central planners. Krainer: "the bankers hold all the power". Dixon: the state is "a piggy bank". Fitts: bankers are taking control of fiscal policy. Berletic: "continuity of agenda". Norton: US policy "is made by and for these powerful corporations". Henningsen: donors choose the cabinet. He says Citibank supplied the short list for Obama's in 2008 and that the Israel lobby "handpicked every cabinet member of the Trump administration that touches national security or foreign policy". Martenson, of Trump: "There's a class of people above him, who have more power than the government because they are completely untouchable." Hudson: "it doesn't matter what the voters say anymore". Crooke: on Russia, Trump is "wholly co-opted". Wilkerson calls the US a "national security state, which we have most assuredly become", and says two White House aides, Stephen Miller and Russell Vought, "are running the freaking government". Diesen: "one can change the people in power, there's often less and less ability to actually change the policies." Webb, quoting the historian Carroll Quigley: "a world system of financial control in private hands". Wolff: both parties are funded by the same employer class, and "The donor class's donations protect it from serious or sustained criticism by either major US Party." Of the four in hidden-layer.md, Dolan: "we're living in an authoritarian system masking as a democratic system"; Greer: "'The Government' - as you and I and Thomas Jefferson may think of it - is really quite outside the loop."
- **Finance has detached from production.** Werner separates credit for production from credit for asset purchases. Hudson, on Norton's site, says the same: banks "lend money for assets... already in existence", not for industrial investment. Krainer frames it as the "British system" against the "American System"; Hudson as finance capitalism against industrial capitalism.
- **Left versus right is the wrong axis.** Werner, Krainer, Dixon, Fitts and Henningsen ("I don't get hung up on the labels") say so directly. Martenson treats the two parties as one. Hudson: "a uniparty with two personalities". Norton and Berletic replace it with empire against the Global South, and Norton adds class.
- **Crises and wars move wealth upward.** Fitts and Dixon describe losses put on the public and gains kept private. Norton: "all US wars are indeed a form of class war". Henningsen: the arms makers are "popping champagne corks" as US missile stocks run down. Wolff, on a declining empire: "The people at the top, the rich and the powerful, respond to the decline by saying, oh, this is terrible, but not for me." Martenson: the Fed is running "a sociological experiment" in moving wealth to the 0.1%. Wilkerson, of the arms makers: "They know they're going to make money out their yin yang."
- **Europe and other US allies are being stripped.** Werner, Berletic, Dixon and Norton all describe this. Noh, cited here as a reference, describes it from inside a US ally: South Korea's army under US wartime command, and a demanded investment equal to its foreign reserves. Henningsen calls the US presence in the Gulf, Korea and Europe "a protection racket", and reads Germany's nuclear shutdown and the loss of Nord Stream as a way to make it dependent. Werner adds Japan, "cannibalized to support the dollar". Hudson: Europe is "committing economic suicide", and the allies are the first target, before the enemies. Wilkerson: "We've already lost 40 and under in Korea." Diesen, a Norwegian: "We are vassals now." Wolff calls Ursula von der Leyen's acceptance of Trump's terms "a craven act of Europe's even more desperate submission".
- **The dollar system is a weapon and is under strain.** Werner on the petrodollar; Norton on the petrodollar and what he calls, after Adam Tooze, the "hedge fund dollar"; Berletic on sanctions and blockades; Dixon on debt rollover; Henningsen on the attack on Iran's currency that he says the US Treasury Secretary boasted of; Martenson on the 2022 freeze of Russia's reserves, after which "people started walking away from the dollar". Hudson: "America has become a pirate state." Crooke: "dollar hegemony largely is a function of preserving the myth of U.S. exceptional military invulnerability". Wolff: "the global understanding of the United States as a place where you can safely keep your money is finished."
Points shared by some but not all:

- **Stablecoins exist to create demand for US government debt.** Fitts, Norton, Werner and Webb all make this point; Webb, with Mark Goodwin, made it first in this set, in July 2024, quoting Tether's chief executive calling it "the best friend of the U.S. government". Werner adds that stablecoins are "a backdoor way" to what he calls "central bank digital control". Hudson adds that the US seized Iran's stablecoin holdings, which makes them "a very unsafe investment" for any state that might cross Washington.
- **Technology is built for war first.** Dixon says this, and Noh, a reference here, in nearly the same words.
- **Headline economic figures mislead.** Fitts on government accounts; Norton on GDP, a tenth of which he says is imputed rent that "does not exist"; Hudson calls much of GDP "a financial illusion"; Martenson on "petroleum" redefined to include gas liquids, which lets the US be called a net exporter.

Seven of the fourteen (Werner, Krainer, Dixon, Fitts, Henningsen, Martenson and Webb) treat digital money and digital ID as the coming instrument of control; Henningsen did so in 2022 and does not return to it in the 2026 material. Seven use the Epstein files as evidence of how compliance is enforced; Wilkerson speculates that Netanyahu holds blackmail material on Trump, and Webb has documented the network at book length. Six frame the struggle as ultimately spiritual; Crooke is the sixth. Wilkerson treats religion in politics as a danger, not a remedy. Norton, Berletic and Hudson do neither, and I found neither in what I read of Henningsen.

## The mechanism, assembled from all the authors

1. **The physical base (Martenson).** All economic activity is work, and work needs energy: there is "no such thing as a low-energy rich country". Money and debt are claims on future output, so they are claims on future energy. Debt compounds; oil and gas do not.
2. **Credit creation (Werner).** Banks create money when they lend. Lending for production gives growth without inflation; lending for asset purchases gives bubbles. Large banks lend to large customers.
3. **Rent (Hudson).** Much of what banks lend against is not production but the right to collect: land rent, monopoly rent and interest. Whatever the tax collector gives up is "available to be paid to the banks as interest", so untaxed rent is turned into debt. He counts this income as overhead, not output.
4. **Ownership (Dixon).** Banks are owned through index funds by a few asset managers, which hold board seats across the market. Compliance comes from the terms of access to capital, not from orders.
5. **Capitalisation (Fitts).** Policy that moves a dollar of income into a listed company creates many dollars of share value, the "pop". Her prison-company case shows a loss-making firm valued at about $24,000 per bed at flotation.
6. **Extraction abroad (Krainer).** Banks need collateral, so control of resources is worth fighting for. Debt and local oligarchs carry wealth from weaker countries to financial centres. Hudson adds the instrument: dollar debts and IMF conditions that make debtor countries sell their infrastructure and resources.
7. **The dollar system (Norton, Hudson).** Oil priced in dollars, surpluses recycled into US Treasuries, and offshore funds buying US debt let the US run deficits the rest of the world finances. Sanctions turn that system into a weapon. Wolff adds why the wars run on borrowing: the US Treasury borrowed the recycled petrodollars "to pay for its many wars, large and small. This enabled US governments to avoid having to raise taxes to pay for wars." He calls the whole arrangement "military Keynesianism": deficit spending on arms as the economy's standing support since 1945, "the perpetual war economy". Hudson's 2026 version: having lost its industry and its creditor position, the US is left with choke points (oil, information technology, shipping routes and the payments system) and uses them to collect tribute.
8. **Enforcement (Berletic, Fitts, Henningsen).** Abroad: sanctions, proxies, blockades and control of sea lanes, set out in advance in policy papers. Henningsen adds two pieces: a repeated "formula" of currency attack, protests and outside provocateurs, which he says he saw in Iran in early 2026 and earlier in Syria; and marine insurance and reinsurance in the City of London, which he places "right up there alongside the central bank" as a lever over shipping. Inside allied countries: command of their armies, pressure on their courts and elections, and coups (Noh, a reference here, on South Korea and Japan). At home: investigations and litigation against insiders who do not cooperate. Webb adds leverage over individuals: Epstein's network tied intelligence services, organised crime, finance and technology, and she argues blackmail was its function.
9. **Consent (Henningsen).** Wars and sanctions need a public story. Media, foundations, NGOs and think tanks supply it, and pundits repeat it. He says of commentators such as Mike Pompeo that they are "paid to have" their views. His 2017 line: "mainstream media, not politics, is the true engine of war". Martenson gives a version with no one giving orders: "I don't believe that there's a smoky room". Reporters know which side their bread is buttered on, and "advertising is bribes". The classic statement is Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model, which lists five filters: ownership, advertising, reliance on official sources, "flak", and anticommunism. Ratigan and Martenson describe the first three without naming it. Diesen gives a documented case from his own country: US embassy cables from Norway, released by WikiLeaks, on working through "our journalists, our NGOs, our think tanks" until Norwegian politicians fell in line. He says European civil society is "managed by 'non-governmental organizations' financed by the US government and cooperating closely with US intelligence services".
10. **Opacity (Fitts, Norton, Martenson).** Government accounts that cannot be audited, a covert budget, and output statistics padded with financial and imputed items. Martenson adds managed prices: futures markets far larger than the physical trade, used as "signaling devices" for gold and now for oil. Wolff gives a live case from October 2026: the refiners warned that if diesel exports were banned they would cut refining "and it'll go right back up", which he calls "a threat by an oligopoly to use its oligopoly to force the prices high": "That's not how the market works. That's how the market is manipulated."
11. **The control layer (Fitts, Werner, Dixon, Martenson).** Programmable money and digital ID would let the same parties set and enforce rules through payments. Martenson and Werner agree on the likeliest first use: fuel rationing in an energy emergency. Webb documents the pieces being built: dollar stablecoins that are "programmable, surveillable" and freeze wallets at the Justice Department's request, the UN's digital-ID target (SDG 16.9), and Pentagon surveillance programmes privatised as Palantir after Congress cut them.
12. **Cohesion (Crooke).** Under the money and the force sits a people's shared sense of who they are. Crooke credits Iran's resistance to "civilisational power", a composite of asymmetric weapons and "altered consciousness", and argues that the West's "moral chaos and fragmentation" is "deliberately fostered" to weaken national and religious attachment.
13. **The hidden layer (Fitts, Greer, Dolan).** Fitts calls it the covert economy. It began with assets seized in the Second World War; the 1949 CIA Act let money be appropriated to agencies and clawed back in secret to a black budget; and in 1981, she says, an executive order let that money hire corporate contractors, so that firms could "end up owning that technology secretly in a way that drives their stock to the moon". Greer describes what he says the money built: "unacknowledged special access projects" whose staff deny their existence even to the president, carried out mostly by contractors ("private industry keeps secrets even better"), with policy resting "predominantly at this time in the private, civilian sector". He put the spending at "some $100 billion per year" in 2004. Dolan names what is at stake: an energy system built on "physical control" of oil and gas is "vulnerable to emerging technologies". Whether the technology came from recovered craft, as Greer and Dolan say, or from human research alone, the financial structure they describe is the same, and it is Fitts's "pop" (part 5) applied to secret work.

Why would anyone suppress growth? There are now three answers. Werner's own, in werner2.txt: since the 1970s central planners have followed the Club of Rome, "we don't really want growth anymore", and "they want to reduce populations everywhere". Fitts, Dixon and Hudson need no such aim: centralisation and financialisation can shrink total output and still enrich those who hold the assets. Martenson's: growth is capped by energy whatever anyone intends. Greer gives a fourth: energy that needs no fuel would let "the have-nots" have, power would "shift - or equalize" to the other 90% of the world, and "those in the cat-bird seat" have "no interest in doing any such thing". On his account the cap Martenson describes is political, not physical.

## What each could teach the others

- **Werner to Krainer and Dixon.** Krainer writes of "fractional reserve lending", and both he and Dixon say the system fails because the interest can never be repaid. Werner's research rejects the fractional-reserve description (`werner1.txt:31-34`) and locates the problem in where credit is directed. Hudson agrees with Werner on this. Martenson makes the same interest argument as Krainer and Dixon while citing Werner as his source on how money is created.
- **Dixon to Werner and Krainer.** Werner's "they" stops at central bankers and an unnamed network. Dixon traces ownership upward and gives a way for coordination to happen without a conspiracy: "This is systemic. There is no one on top. It's not an organized cabal."
- **Norton to Krainer and Fitts.** Norton's rulers are not hidden. He points to the oligarchs seated at the White House table and to policy written down in the National Security Strategy. If power is exercised that openly, a hidden hand is not needed to explain it.
- **Norton to the others on numbers.** He does not discard official statistics. He reads them against each other: median wealth against average, what is counted in GDP, health outcomes against spending. That is a way to use figures without trusting them blindly.
- **Fitts to the others.** She has company filings, audit reports and first-hand experience of enforcement. Krainer writes that "we can't expect to find documented evidence"; her prison chapters are evidence of the kind he says is unavailable.
- **Berletic to Krainer, Werner and Norton.** His method is to read the policy papers and assume continuity. It predicted Trump's course better than Krainer or Werner did. He also warns that the US advances its aims even when it appears to lose, which is a caution for Norton's and Krainer's accounts of American defeat.
- **Noh (a reference) to the others on reading documents.** His guide to the National Security Strategy says to read the whole text, look for contradictions, and separate "remarks" (rhetoric) from "code" (policy already in motion). He warns against readings coloured by "wishful thinking, obsessive paranoia, axes-to-grind". That is a discipline several of the others could use.
- **Noh (a reference) to Werner.** Werner says the 1997 Asian crisis was used to change ownership. Noh gives the same event from the receiving end in South Korea, and adds that Japan's economy was "manipulated by the US", which is the outside half of Werner's story about the Bank of Japan.
- **Henningsen to Berletic.** Asked about Berletic's reading that the chaos is intended, Henningsen replied: "if this is some grandmaster plan, can someone explain to me what is in America's interest... Because I personally cannot see it." He points to who does gain: arms makers, who profit while "accelerating the demise of the very empire" they serve, and Israel. My inference: this lets a war be planned and self-defeating at once, because the parts of the system want different things.
- **Henningsen to Krainer.** Krainer puts the City of London at the centre. Henningsen offers specific levers: old money and foreign money ("the colonial dividend"), the insurers and reinsurers ("the names"), and a Britain that "excels in disciplining the United States, nudging the United States" through intelligence. He still ranks them differently: "The US empire is the dog."
- **Henningsen to Dixon and Fitts.** In 2022 he wrote that the two blocs are diverging, with Russia and China leading Eurasia one way "while Klaus and the West head towards their technocratic digital 'utopia'". That is a direct answer to Dixon's claim that the multipolar world is the same capital's next home.
- **Henningsen to all on the press.** He treats a leak as an act: who placed it, and whom it is meant to pressure. He reads a Reuters report of secret US talks with Yemen as a message to Saudi Arabia. Several of the others quote mainstream admissions without asking that question.
- **Werner and Dixon to Henningsen.** His account of money is among the thinnest here. "The bankers seem to always come out of top" is where it stops. Werner's credit mechanism and Dixon's ownership chain would give it content.
- **Noh (a reference) to Henningsen.** In 2017 Henningsen wrote that North Korea had "no discernible nuclear weapons capability". Noh treats the deterrent as real and central. By 2026 Henningsen is close to Noh: the US mission in Korea is "a protection racket" and Seoul is turning toward a settlement with the North.
- **Martenson to Werner, and back.** This is the one direct exchange in the set. Werner says high growth would make the debt "a secondary issue". Martenson put his energy chart to him: growth needs energy, and US oil and gas are near their peak. Werner answered that oil may be renewed inside the earth and "perhaps we're not beyond... peak oil". Martenson wrote afterwards that "abiotic oil is just not a significant part of the story". Neither moved.
- **Martenson to Berletic and Henningsen.** He puts numbers on the energy loss they describe: 86 million barrels a day before the war, about 12 million missing, which is the 2011 level, when the world economy was 36% smaller.
- **Martenson to all, on stating what would prove you wrong.** "Either our systems of money and economy are either about to crash, and crash hard, or the world is not nearly as dependent on energy as I think it is." None of the others states a test of their own thesis that plainly.
- **Hudson and Werner, on the same evidence.** Both say banks lend for assets instead of production, and both hold up China. They draw opposite lessons. Werner wants thousands of small local banks and fears central planners. Hudson wants money and credit to be "a public utility" under "a central authority to oversee the financial system", and credits the People's Bank of China. I found no exchange between them.
- **Hudson to Krainer.** Krainer asserts a hidden hand. Hudson gives the same oligarchy a documented origin (the papacy's contracts with warlords and bankers to fund the Crusades, then "fiscal states" run for their creditors) and says of today's plan: "There is no shame in this, no attempt to conceal it."
- **Hudson to Martenson.** Both expect the oil cut to bring a depression on the scale of the 1930s or worse, and both say market prices are being managed. They part on what to do. Martenson calls taxing wealth "violence" and builds personal buffers. Hudson would tax land and monopoly rent and write down debts that cannot be paid, as rulers did from Babylon to 1931.
- **Hudson to Dixon and Fitts on crypto.** The US seized Iran's stablecoin holdings. Bitcoin has not been seized so far, but he calls it "a transitory system". That is evidence for Fitts's view that the rails are controlled, and a limit on Dixon's exit.
- **Wilkerson to all, on the whistleblower question.** He is the nearest thing to an insider who broke ranks in this set. As Powell's chief of staff he says the Bush administration "committed war crimes" and that torture "broke my back". What he describes from inside is not a hidden directing body. It is careerism (flag officers who "covet that slot" at Lockheed Martin or RTX), donors, ideology, and hatred: "There is no strategic purpose to it." That supports Dixon and the nexus model against Krainer's hidden hand.
- **Wilkerson to Berletic.** Berletic reads the war as a planned step toward China. Wilkerson agrees that blocking China's railway through Iran is a real motive ("that railroad will take 60% of the commerce that China now generates... off the seas"), but adds: "I'm not sure Trump's aware of it." A plan can live in the planners without the president.
- **Crooke to the finance writers.** He reverses their order. The "financial oligarchy" is the "enforcement structure" of a "dark state" bid for global primacy, and that bid rests on a cultural collapse. For Werner, Krainer, Dixon, Fitts and Hudson, finance is the top.
- **Crooke to Henningsen and Berletic.** He holds both readings of the war at once: it is waged "primordially" for Israeli hegemony, and it is "all about a bigger U.S. play" to control chokepoints and deny China energy.
- **Webb to Werner, on March 2020.** Werner says the Fed adopted BlackRock's "go direct" plan in March 2020 and hired BlackRock to carry it out. Webb and Goodwin document the same sequence: BlackRock proposed it in August 2019, the Fed hired it to run the facilities, and it bought its own funds with the money. They draw on John Titus's work for Fitts's Solari Report, so this is not independent of Fitts, but it is independent of Werner.
- **Webb to Dixon and Krainer, on exits.** Dixon calls Bitcoin in self-custody the exit; Krainer holds a token. Webb calls this "decentralization theater" that is "masking the greatest centralization of power in history". She and Goodwin argue Bitcoin is being folded into the dollar through US-custodied funds and becomes a sink for dollar inflation.
- **Webb to the multipolar camp.** She cites Samuel Pisar's 1971 testimony on the "transideological corporation": Western firms merging with the state firms of the East into one system of private financial control. That is the strongest version of Dixon's answer to open question 6, and the opposite of Diesen's and Henningsen's.
- **Diesen to all, on how to argue.** "Morality entails a commitment to principles about what is right and wrong, while moralism uses moral principles to judge, condemn, and police others." Read as a rule for this group: argue about interests and evidence, not about the other author's character.

## Open questions for the room

1. What separates a sovereign state that disciplines finance from a control grid? Nobody has tested that answer against Fitts's and Dixon's fears. 3. Is the root problem interest-bearing money, or the direction of credit? Martenson now sits with Krainer and Dixon on this, against Werner, whom he cites.
4. Is anyone hidden at the top? Dixon says incentives; Norton says an open class. What does Krainer's hidden hand add? A candidate answer follows this list.

6. Is the multipolar bloc a different system, or the next home of the same capital? Henningsen and Dixon give opposite answers. Webb's "transideological" system is the strongest "same capital" answer; Diesen's balance of power is the strongest "different system" answer.

9. Can growth solve the debt, as Werner says, or is growth capped by energy, as Martenson says? They discussed it once and did not settle it. The answer decides whether Werner's remedy can work.
10. Is the system run by a plan, by interests, or by ideology and hatred? Wilkerson, who saw it from inside, gives more weight to the last than anyone else here.
11. Can a remedy be built from money and institutions alone, or does it need what Crooke calls a people's "consciousness"? None of the finance writers addresses this.
12. Is there a second technology and a second economy? Fitts, Greer and Dolan say yes; the rest of the fourteen do not address it. If Greer is right about energy, Martenson's limit is political and the oil choke points are temporary. What would settle it, short of a working device in public? Greer says he spent $900,000 looking for one, and "Dozens of times we have gotten close to finding such a system- only to have it vanish."

## A candidate answer to question 4: a nexus of interests

This model was proposed by the person who assembled these sources. The restatement, the objections and the test are mine.

> The "US empire" is not a country. It is a set of interest blocs that share the use of the US state: finance, arms, energy, technology and data, the Israel lobby, and the media and think tanks that sell their policies. Each bloc is strongest in its own domain. No bloc controls everything. A policy happens when several blocs want it and none with the power to block it objects. The blocs serve themselves, not the United States, so a policy can enrich them and weaken the country at once. Finance is not one bloc among equals: it owns and funds the others, so it sets the terms they compete on.

Where each piece comes from:

- **No one on top.** Dixon: "This is systemic. There is no one on top. It's not an organized cabal."
- **Finance as a layer, not a peer.** Dixon's ownership chain puts the same asset managers behind the arms, energy and technology firms. Fitts's "pop" shows why all of them answer to share price. The blocs are separate when they lobby and joined where they are owned.
- **Technology and consent as blocs.** Henningsen says big AI and big data are "part and parcel of the military-industrial complex", and makes media and think tanks the means by which the others get their wars accepted.
- **The Israel lobby as one bloc with a domain.** Henningsen says it steers US policy in West Asia. Berletic, Dixon and Hudson say Israel is the proxy. The model accommodates both: the lobby wins where arms and energy also gain.
- **Choke points as the blocs' shared method.** Hudson lists four monopolies the US state now enforces: oil, information technology, shipping routes and payments. They map onto the energy, technology and finance blocs.
- **Chomsky as the reference case.** His 2006 argument applies the same logic to Israel. Where the lobby's aims match the "strategic-economic interests of concentrations of domestic power", the two cannot be told apart; where they diverged, Israel backed down. That is the test proposed below, already run on earlier cases.
- **A religious bloc.** Crooke describes a messianic current in Israel's government; Wilkerson describes Christian nationalists being placed through the US officer corps. Neither fits a model of interests alone.
- **Structure, not conspiracy.** Diesen: a hegemon without a rival will expand until it exhausts itself, and "The consequence was predictable." Coordination comes from position, not instruction.
- **The beneficiary is the bloc, not the country.** Henningsen: arms makers profit while "accelerating the demise of the very empire".
- **Blocs bargaining over the state (Wolff).** In October 2026 he described government as the arena in which "clumps" of capitalists "work out amongst themselves a deal". The military-industrial complex showed the way ("don't make a monopoly... Have three or four an oligopoly" and "go as a coordinated group to the Congress"); the "medical industrial complex" copied it, and now high tech. His case of the moment: farmers wanted a ban on diesel exports, the oil companies did not, and the oil companies won. "If we were watching this in Russia, there would be all this conversation about oligarchs because that's what we have." That is the nexus model from the left, with the blocs fighting each other.
- **A hidden bloc.** Greer describes the policy group behind the secret programs as "some 200–300 individuals from a number of countries", representing "financial, technological, security, religious, media, political and scientific" interests. That is the nexus with a religious bloc added, and with technology the public has not seen.

What the model explains:

- Why no whistleblower describes a central directing body: there is none to describe. This matches the insiders listed above, each of whom describes one part.
- Why some wars are not meant to be won: the contract is the goal.
- Why policy looks incoherent, as in Trump's reversals: the blocs disagree.
- Why Berletic finds continuity in policy papers: the think tanks that write them are funded by the blocs, so the plans are real without a single planner.

Who would object:

- **Krainer, in part.** On who is on top the model agrees with him: finance, above the other blocs. Two differences remain. He describes a "monolithic and ruthless conspiracy" where the model needs only shared ownership and incentives. And he treats the other blocs as instruments (he calls Israel "the invention of the Brits"), where the model lets them win on their own account.
- **Berletic.** The chaos is the plan, and it serves the US against China.
- **Norton.** The blocs are one class, so "nexus" overstates how separate they are.
- **Wilkerson, in part.** From inside he saw interests at work, but also hatred with "no strategic purpose". A model built only on interests misses that.
- **Crooke.** He would say the model describes the enforcers and leaves out the project they serve.
- **Webb.** She documents the people who join the blocs: the same financiers, intelligence veterans and technology investors appear in all of them. To her, that is a decades-long plan by a small group, not a nexus of separate interests.
- **Greer.** He would accept the blocs but say the strongest one is hidden: an entity with "more power than any single government in the world or any single identified world leader", which the visible blocs serve without knowing it.

A test: the model predicts that policy stalls or reverses where the blocs conflict. The case to watch is one where the Israel lobby wants something that finance or energy loses from. If the lobby still wins, the model is wrong and Henningsen's stronger claim, that the war serves Israel alone, is right. The same test separates the model from Krainer: if finance always prevails over the other blocs, his account of them as instruments fits better.

A first case, from Wolff (October 2026), shows two blocs colliding: big agriculture against big oil, over diesel.
- **The collision.** Diesel prices were "literally extinguishing the margin" for farming, so the farm-state senators and the large agricultural corporations behind them asked Trump to ban diesel exports. The refiners objected: "We have been your loyal supporters. We don't want this."
- **The broker.** Trump floated the ban and repeated it, caught between the two: "One group of his supporters want it, another group of his supporters don't want it." Wolff describes his method as "to try to give to whoever comes to him what they want".
- **The outcome.** The refiners warned that with a ban "we will cut back on refining and it'll go right back up." The ban was dropped. The farmers got a release of European diesel reserves which, Wolff says, equals about "one day's demand".

My reading: this is what the model predicts. When the blocs conflicted, policy stalled, the older and better-organised bloc won, and the weaker one got a token. It is one case, told by one author, and Wolff's verdict on the outcome is "That seems to have worked", not a confirmed result. It does not test the harder case, the Israel lobby against finance or energy. It also bears on two forecasts: Hudson's, that Trump would hold fuel prices down until the November vote, and Martenson's, that oil would be pushed down before the midterms. A refiners' veto on cheaper diesel cuts against both; the November vote will show which held.

## A candidate answer to question 8: energy dominance by managed demolition

This hypothesis was proposed by the person who assembled these sources. The restatement, the evidence from the authors, the objections and the tests are mine.

> The Iran war is not what it looks like. The aim is not to win in the Gulf but to close it, with deniability, and let Iran take the blame. With Gulf oil and gas impaired, Europe and Asia depend on US energy, which is also the most profitable outcome for the US energy bloc. Bases in the Middle East that Iran can predictably destroy are given up, while attention moves to the Arctic, where Russia and China are opening new sea lanes. The same pattern ran earlier through Ukraine: Russian energy infrastructure struck, Nord Stream destroyed, Europe made dependent on US gas. Israel's interests run the same way: Gulf disruption favours routes through the Mediterranean, offshore gas including the field off Gaza, and a canal to rival Suez. Deals to reopen the Gulf will keep failing, because reopening it is not the goal. Even large damage to the world economy is acceptable if the US stays on top a little longer.

What the authors already say:

- **Berletic** is closest to the whole thesis.
  - "The war on Iran is part of a decades-spanning US project to assume complete control over the Middle East and the oil and gas that is produced and exported".
  - The war "may represent a broader strategy aimed at reshaping global energy flows".
  - It is "placing Europe under increasing and likely irreversible energy dependence on the United States", and "This energy dependence on the US obviously benefits US-based energy corporations financially".
  - On Europe: "the destruction of the Nord Stream pipelines and ever-tightening sanctions on Russian energy exports that have made otherwise irrational US LNG imports to Europe essential".
  - One of his titles: "Why the War Might Pause but Won't End".
- **Hudson** calls it an oil war over choke points. Voicing Washington's logic: "we have Venezuela's oil now. We have Canada's oil. We can block the trade for Russian oil through the Arctic by invading Greenland". On a settlement: "There's no deal that's imminent at all, and there cannot be."
- **Wilkerson** on the bases: "Mark my words, we're leaving." On Israel's gas: "the oil field and gas field they stole in the Mediterranean from the Gazans".
- **Wolff** on the Gulf bases: "The bases and their host nations have become endangered targets vulnerable to Iranian drones and missiles." The diesel case above shows the US energy bloc winning when its interests are at stake.
- **Against it:** Henningsen says the war "has nothing to do with US interests. It's a war for Israel." Wolff, Dolan, Crooke and Diesen read it as a failed gamble. In Wolff's words, its "goal was to reverse the decline of the US empire; its result was the opposite."

Facts checked against outside reporting (October 2026):
- **The Ben-Gurion Canal.** A real proposal, about $55 billion, from Eilat to the Mediterranean. Interest revived in March 2026; nothing has been built.
- **Gaza Marine.** A real field of about 1 trillion cubic feet, found in 2000. Israel approved its development in 2023; it stalled after 7 October.
- **Licences.** In October 2023, during the Gaza war, Israel awarded offshore gas exploration licences to BP, Eni, Socar and others.
- **Conoco.** I found no Conoco contract; this claim still needs a source.

The pieces exist. What is not yet shown is that they were the war's purpose rather than opportunities taken along the way.

What the hypothesis explains:
- **Why no deal holds.** Hudson's and Berletic's readings agree: failure is built in.
- **The Iran war is not a test of bloc conflict.** This bears on the test proposed for the nexus model. If Israel's and the energy bloc's interests run together, the war is not a collision between them, and the model predicts that policy moves at full speed when the blocs align.

Objections:
- **It can explain any outcome.** If every failure was intended, nothing could count against the theory. It needs the tests below.
- **The US pays too.** Wolff's "fiscal cliff", Hudson's expected depression, and above all the dollar. Wolff expects the Gulf states to be "much less of a buyer of American debt". That is a loss for the finance bloc and the Treasury market. On the nexus model, the energy bloc's gain and the finance bloc's loss is itself a collision to watch: if finance loses here, Krainer's finance-on-top view is weakened; if the Gulf reopens because finance pushes for it, the hypothesis is weakened.

Tests, entered in the ledger as forecasts:
1. **US share of gas imports.** The US share of Europe's and Asia's gas imports rises and stays high, with long-term contracts signed.
2. **Mediterranean routes.** Concrete progress on a Mediterranean route: financing or ground broken on a pipeline or the canal, or development of Gaza Marine.
3. **Gulf supply.** Gulf supply stays impaired while US, Canadian and Venezuelan supply fills the gap.
4. **Bases.** US bases in the Gulf are reduced while Arctic deployments grow.
5. **A settlement.** No durable settlement reopens Hormuz; if one does, and Gulf flows recover quickly, the hypothesis weakens.

## Practical convergence

Fitts and Dixon give the most personal advice, and it overlaps: stop shopping with and banking at the big firms (Fitts: "You can use cash... You can stop using the banks"; Dixon: "Boycott more this month than last month"), spend and invest locally, and hold assets no one can switch off. Werner argues for small local banks, but as policy rather than personal advice. Krainer has personal advice too: take money out of the banking system and put it to productive use, ideally by "investing in diversified farming operations in exchange for a share of the farm's produce", which "can bypass the banking system completely" (July 2026). In 2023 he advised farmland, gold and silver, and commodity futures; by 2026 he calls such holdings "merely temporary refuges". Wolff's advice is to organise: "Build on what you got... Work with us, with everybody else", and in the longer run to turn workplaces into worker cooperatives. Krainer, Norton and Hudson add the national programme: public credit and capital directed to production, and for Hudson a tax on land rent and a write-down of unpayable debt. Henningsen adds energy independence as a condition of sovereignty, and a reading habit: ask who pays the person speaking and who placed the story. Crooke adds food self-sufficiency at national level. Diesen adds talking to the opponent, which European governments refuse to do. Webb gives no personal programme in what I read; her contribution is exposure. Wilkerson adds an amendment to undo Citizens United, which he says is moving through the states. Martenson calls his version "buffers": some food, water and energy of your own, gold and land, and neighbours you know. As Dixon's host put it, the personal advice holds even if the larger account is wrong.

My inference: the fourteen agree that power sits above elected government and broadly on how it works, and disagree on identity and remedy, so the mechanism is the firmest part of their combined account. The disagreement on remedy matters more than the disagreement on identity, because it decides what people are asked to do.
