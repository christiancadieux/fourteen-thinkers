So, what are these two opposed systems of economic governance? One is the British system of free trade. The other is the national system of political economy. It is often referred to as the American system because it was pioneered by American leaders like Alexander Hamilton, Henry Clay, Abraham Lincoln, and Lincoln’s chief economic advisor Henry C. Carey.

In a nutshell, the national system seeks to create protective barriers to foreign trade in order to give the domestic manufacturing a fighting chance against foreign competition. It also accumulates capital at home, investing it into manufacturing, infrastructure, education, research and development, innovation, culture and other uses that raise the society’s living standards.

By contrast, the British system of free trade extracts wealth and concentrates it: it seeks to remove all barriers to worldwide free flow of capital, always in pursuit of highest possible returns. With free trade, nations are obliged to compete for investment capital by systematically depressing wages, job security, pensions, health care services and education.

Other costs which cut into the returns on capital are infrastructure development, social spending programs and protection of the environment. Therefore, competing globally for investment capital entails progressive slashing of all these cost items, down to the bare bones. In that sense, British free trade system amounts to a competitive race to the bottom.

In his 1851 book titled, “The Harmony of Interests,” Abraham Lincoln’s economic advisor Henry Carey contrasted the two systems, opening with the words, “Two systems are before the world…”

Carey’s prose is rather long, but it could be summarized along seven key dimensions:

1. Trade vs. production: one system directs labor and capital toward trade and speculation. This tends to depress wages and erode prosperity. The other system fosters production of goods, services and infrastructure. It leads to higher wages and rising standards of living.

2. Raw materials vs. finished goods: one system favors mass production of raw materials and cash crops for wholesale trade. It systematically impoverishes farmers and reduces their employment. The other system favors production of more refined, high quality products, enriching their producers.

3. Exporting produce vs. enjoying it at home: one system sends mass produced bulk abroad, sometimes even causing famines in the producing economy. The other system organizes labor to satisfy domestic demand and produce an abundance of diverse, high quality products at home.

4. Concentration vs. dispersion of wealth: one system concentrates wealth and power in few hands, in great commercial cities while depleting and impoverishing the rest of the nation; the other system enriches those engaged in production and disperses wealth more evenly across society.

5. Military spending vs. moral and intellectual growth: one system compels the productive sector to fund fleets and armies; the other directs the same means for moral and intellectual growth of the people in their own nations.

6. Stable communities vs. population replacement: one system requires a large, low-skilled labor force that accepts work with low wages. It predictably resorts to mass influx of migrants. The other system raises the standards of living, prosperity and security of the native labor force.

7. Universal war vs. universal peace: one system gives rise to universal forever wars; the other strives to preserve peace.

Let’s condense these seven points further: one system favors production of raw commodities in bulk. It lowers living standards, concentrates wealth in few hands, impoverishes the workforce and favors population replacement, militarization and forever wars. This is the British system of free trade.

The other system favors production of diverse, quality products at home, enriches their producers, raises living standards, disperses wealth, fosters stable communities and favors stability and peace. This is the national system of political economy, or the American system. Its aspiration is sovereignty and independence. Free trade system’s aspiration is globalization and unipolar hegemony.

The contrast between the two systems could not be any starker, and the record entirely validates the way Henry Carey characterized them, as we’ll see from actual historical examples where one system or the other was implemented, and from the ways they changed the societies in question.

Free trade system is the economic and intellectual heritage of European colonial empires. It was refined and perfected by the stewards of the British Empire. After the defeat of Napoleon Bonaparte at Waterloo in 1815, Britain eliminated its greatest European rival and was now in position to consolidate its power globally and lay the groundwork for a unipolar global order under her own dominance.

Only one year after Waterloo, on 22^(nd) June 1816, British parliament enacted a law declaring gold to be the sole measure of value in the Empire. The ensuing scramble for gold largely shaped British foreign policy over the following eight decades. Its imperative was to ensure that the newly mined gold from Australia, California and South Africa flowed into the vaults of the Bank of England while denying the same, as much as possible, to all other nations, including the United States.

That required establishing naval supremacy across the world’s oceans. Britain’s dominance in shipping, maritime insurance and control over key ports, trade routes and maritime chokepoints enabled her to limit other powers’ access to global markets. British ships carried gold and silver, as well as steel, coal and other commodities from her colonies back to England while the products of English industries sailed to foreign markets around the world.

In the process, Britain accumulated the world’s largest hoard of monetary gold. Indeed, the stunning success of this colossal-scale looting operation also shaped Britain’s internal economic and social policies. The program was working or, at least it was working for the British ruling classes, so it had to be enshrined, protected and defended.

In 1820, under the influence of a powerful group of London merchants and bankers concentrated around the Bank of England, British parliament passed a Statement of Principle in support of so-called “absolute free trade.” The idea was formulated a few decades earlier by the Scottish economist Adam Smith and a banking oligarch, David Ricardo.

Under this system, Britain’s bulging accumulation of capital would not be squandered on domestic economic development or improvements for the ordinary British people. Instead, it would be invested into enhancing the original looting operation by expanding Britain’s colonial conquests and global free trade.

In 1843, some 23 years after absolute free trade was enshrined as the empire’s official policy, its main beneficiaries founded a weekly propaganda journal, The Economist, whose explicit purpose was to promote and reinforce the intellectual foundations supporting the free trade system.

In large part, this was necessary to prepare the grounds for an assault on British workers and their communities. Namely, depressing the cost of labor was part and parcel of the free trade system, so any protections that British workers and farmers enjoyed, had to be eliminated. Britain’s domestic markets would have to be opened wide to cheap imports from around the world, which crushed local producers and devastated the domestic economy.

For example, in 1846, the Parliament repealed the so-called “Corn Laws,” which had protected British farmers from foreign imports. After their repeal, African or Hindu farmers, working for slave-level wages, could compete against British and Irish farmers for the market of British “consumers.”

The result was that the prices of produce, wage levels and living standards in Britain began to collapse and the society started to rot from within. “Where the aristocracy is richer and more powerful than [in] any other country in the world, the poor are more oppressed, more pauperized, more numerous in comparison to the other classes, more irreligious and very much worse educated than the poor of any other European nation…”

At the façade, the British system seemed unassailable, but its internal rot, radical polarization of wealth and increasing allocation of that wealth to speculative ends and warfare, ultimately led to its collapse. As I mentioned above, London’s gold hoard was largely invested toward enhancing its colossal looting operation, into new ventures abroad.

As a result, the gold stored in London vaults wasn’t nearly sufficient to meet the ballooning claims against it. In 1857 a banking panic erupted, triggering a run on Bank of England’s gold reserves, which in turn collapsed bank credit across the country and precipitated an economic depression. As devastating as that was, it was only the beginning of Britain’s troubles.

The policies pursued by the City of London bankers weren’t concerned with the economic recovery for the nation. Instead, they obsessed about reversing the outflow of gold and returning the bullion into the vaults of London banks and the Bank of England. To achieve that, the bankers started raising interest rates.

The idea was to incentivize investors around the world to bring their gold back to England and exchange it for the interest-bearing financial instruments. In that sense, the policy was successful, but it came with a huge cost for the British economy and for the ordinary Britons.

High interest rates caused a wave of business bankruptcies and foreclosures. The financial bubble that emerged from speculation on railways construction in North and South Americas suddenly collapsed, and by 1873 Britain fell into The Great Depression which would last for 23 years, until 1896.
