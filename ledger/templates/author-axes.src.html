Where they stand decides what they see

The sixteen look at the same object, US power and its wars, from different vantage points. A realist sees states, a former banker sees credit, an investigative journalist sees networks. Each row below is one author and each column one vantage point, a side of the object; a bigger dot means more of the author's work is seen from there. Most authors look from more than one.

Power sits in statesPower sits in networks above states

The rows are ordered by the authors' conclusions, not by where they look: dimension 1 of the map, from power sitting in states (blue, top) to power sitting in networks above states (orange, bottom). The dots still run corner to corner. Where an author stands largely predicts what they conclude.

The exceptions are worth a look. Berletic looks mostly at energy and weapons, and Norton at class, yet both place power in states: Berletic reads corporate interests through state policy, and Norton applies class analysis to states and blocs. Hudson and Wolff look at money and class and land in the middle.

Read this way, many disagreements are two people describing different sides of the same thing. Each author is most reliable on the side they know best.

The weights are my judgement from each author's background and texts, not a measurement. Hover over a dot for the reason.

One map of all sixteen

Each author's thirteen scores, reduced to the three directions in which the authors differ most (principal component analysis). Authors with similar views sit close together. Choose any two of the three dimensions for the axes; each is named after the questions that drive it, listed under the map.

What the percentages mean

Each author is scored on thirteen questions. The map does not show two of those questions. Each of its three dimensions combines all thirteen, weighted toward questions on which the authors' scores tend to move together. Dimension 1, for example, is mostly where power sits, the remedy, who the actors are, whether finance is the seat of power, and the multipolar bloc: authors who score high on one of these tend to score high, or consistently low, on the others, so a single dimension can summarise all five. Not every difference between the authors follows such a shared pattern, so some is always lost. The percentage says how much of the total variation in the authors' scores each dimension keeps: dimension 1 keeps 36%, dimension 2 adds 24%, and dimension 3 adds 12%, so the three together keep 71%. The other 29% does not show on the map.

The three dimensions, and the questions each is mostly made of:

    What gets lost is mostly a view that one author holds alone. Krainer is the clearest example. He went furthest in expecting Trump to be a real break: he called him "real, not controlled, opposition", and he is the only author to score 1 on that question, where eight of the others score 4 or 5. Because no one else goes that far, and the authors who lean his way on Trump disagree with him on most other questions, that view lines up only weakly with the three dimensions shown. Most of it falls in a fourth, smaller dimension that the map leaves out, so the map barely shows it. On most other questions Krainer agrees with Hudson and Norton, and the map places him in the same upper group as them, which is right on most questions but hides that one.

    So read the map this way: authors who sit close together agree on most questions, but not necessarily on every one, and an author who stands apart on a single question can look closer to the group than he is. For the exact score on any one question, with the author's own words, use the question charts in Where each author sits.

    Some authors have no score on a few questions; there the question's average is used. Webb and Mearsheimer, marked * on the map, are missing three or more, so their positions are rougher. The dimension names are a reading of the questions they are made of.

    How much of each author is quoted

    The number of quotations from each author’s own words that back these pages: in the text of synthesis.md, and in the reasons and advice on this page. Every one is checked against the author’s saved texts by the ledger build. More quotes means more of an author’s position rests on their own wording rather than on my summary; it also reflects how much of their material I could read. Terms of one or two words (Fitts’s “pop”, “come clean”, “total malice”) are too short to check and are not counted, which is why her synthesis.md count is low.

    Who sits with whom

    Average gap between each pair of authors, in scale points. Darker cells are closer.

    • Two camps. The first trusts states: Norton, Hudson and Berletic, with Henningsen, Crooke, Wilkerson, Wolff, Diesen, Mearsheimer and Sachs close by. The second puts power in networks above countries and fears the state's tools: Werner, Fitts, Martenson and Webb, with Dixon further out.
    • The three academics are the tightest cluster. Diesen, Mearsheimer and Sachs make up the three closest pairs in the set. They are the realist pole.
    • A third line: countries or blocs. On the two newest questions, Diesen, Mearsheimer, Sachs, Wilkerson and Crooke stand apart. They treat states and leaders as the actors and wars as won or lost by countries, while nearly everyone else sees blocs or classes, and complexes that gain win or lose.
    • Krainer bridges the first two camps. He puts finance above all countries, as the second camp does, and wants a strong state, as the first does.
    • Henningsen is the most central, then Wolff, Hudson and Crooke. They sit closest to the group's middle answer across the questions. Hudson's closest neighbour is Norton, whose site carries Hudson's interviews, so some of Norton's positions here are Hudson's own words.
    • Sachs and Dixon are furthest apart, then Webb with Sachs and with Mearsheimer. Sachs and Mearsheimer see states and leaders making the choices; Dixon and Webb see states, the multipolar ones included, as instruments of private networks.
    • Dixon stands furthest from the crowd, then Webb, Mearsheimer and Sachs. Dixon and Webb put power above countries; Mearsheimer and Sachs are the outliers at the other end, the state frame.

    Weighing the evidence: what is the Iran war for, and is it working?

    The charts compare the authors’ positions. This section asks which explanation fits the facts best. Facts come first: documents, public data and what authors report as having happened, not their interpretations. Each fact is marked as fitting an explanation, ruling against it, or not bearing on it. The best explanation is the one with the fewest solid facts against it. Facts that could be staged for appearances are flagged, and the ranking is shown both ways.

    There are two questions, because a war can have a purpose and still fail. The first grid asks what the war is for. The second assumes a purposeful plan and asks whether it is working.

    Hypotheses

    The authors’ disagreements come down to a few competing explanations. Each card gives the claim, whose material fits it or cuts against it, what would test it, and where it stands. All come from the authors’ material; four (H1, H2, H9, H10) combine pieces from several authors into one model, and each card names whose pieces they are. The same list closes synthesis.md, and both are generated from the ledger.

    So far the forecast record favours the readings built on written plans and on structure (H6, H5) over readings of Trump’s character; one collision between blocs, the diesel case, fits H1 and H4.

    How to read this

    • A score is a position, not a grade. A 5 is not better than a 1.
    • Scores are whole numbers on a five-point scale, so a one-point gap is within my margin of judgement. Gaps of two or more are the ones to trust.
    • The margin and pair figures treat all thirteen axes as equally important. Change that and the ranking can move.
    • Left out because fewer than ten authors take a position, or because the answer is yes or no: crypto as exit or trap (four authors), depopulation (raised by Krainer, Fitts and Werner only), the Epstein files, a spiritual framing, whether Western workers gain or lose, Martenson's question of whether energy caps growth, and Hudson's of whether debts should be written down.
    • Berletic, Wilkerson, Diesen and Wolff have no score on digital control; Webb has none on China, the Iran war’s target, elections or who gains from the wars; Krainer none on the Iran war’s target; Martenson none on the multipolar bloc; Werner and Norton none on elections. I found no position in what I read.
    • Chomsky and K.J. Noh are cited in the synthesis as references and are not scored here: Chomsky has nothing on 2026, and Noh’s positions largely duplicate Norton’s, whose site he writes for.
