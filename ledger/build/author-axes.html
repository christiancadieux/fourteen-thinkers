Sixteen Authors, Thirteen Axes

Each author is placed from 1 to 5 on thirteen questions where at least ten of the sixteen take a position. The scores are my reading of the synthesis document and the authors’ texts, not measurements. The last three questions were added in the tenth pass. The reason for every score is listed under its chart. The full written synthesis, with the sources, the forecasts and the hypotheses, is in synthesis.md.

How much of each author is quoted

The number of quotations from each author’s own words that back these pages: in the text of synthesis.md, and in the reasons and advice on this page. Every one is checked against the author’s saved texts by the ledger build. More quotes means more of an author’s position rests on their own wording rather than on my summary; it also reflects how much of their material I could read. Terms of one or two words (Fitts’s “pop”, “come clean”, “total malice”) are too short to check and are not counted, which is why her synthesis.md count is low.

Who sits with whom

Average gap between each pair of authors, in scale points. Darker cells are closer.

  • Two camps. The first trusts states: Norton, Hudson and Berletic, with Henningsen, Crooke, Wilkerson, Wolff, Diesen, Mearsheimer and Sachs close by. The second puts power in networks above countries and fears the state's tools: Werner, Fitts, Martenson and Webb, with Dixon further out.
  • The three academics are the tightest cluster. Diesen, Mearsheimer and Sachs make up the three closest pairs in the set. They are the realist pole, and they appear on the same shows, often together, so their closeness is not independent agreement.
  • A third line: countries or blocs. On the two newest questions, Diesen, Mearsheimer, Sachs, Wilkerson and Crooke stand apart. They treat states and leaders as the actors and wars as won or lost by countries, while nearly everyone else sees blocs or classes, and complexes that gain win or lose.
  • Krainer bridges the first two camps. He puts finance above all countries, as the second camp does, and wants a strong state, as the first does.
  • Henningsen is the most central, then Wolff, Hudson and Crooke. They sit closest to the group's middle answer across the questions. Hudson's closest neighbour is Norton, whose site carries his interviews, and Wolff's is Wilkerson, whose shows he shares, so those closenesses are not independent agreement.
  • Sachs and Dixon are furthest apart, then Webb with Sachs and with Mearsheimer. Sachs and Mearsheimer see states and leaders making the choices; Dixon and Webb see states, the multipolar ones included, as instruments of private networks.
  • Dixon stands furthest from the crowd, then Webb, Mearsheimer and Sachs. Dixon and Webb put power above countries; Mearsheimer and Sachs are the outliers at the other end, the state frame.

Do two disagreements line up?

Each question above shows one disagreement. This chart puts two questions side by side, to show whether they are really the same disagreement. Pick a question for each direction.

  • If the authors fall along a diagonal, the two questions split them the same way, so they are one disagreement asked twice. Try What is the remedy? against How do they see China?: those who want a strong sovereign state also see China favourably, and those who want to decentralise and exit do not.
  • If they spread into the corners, the questions cut across each other, and the corners are the real groupings. The default view, Who are the actors? against What is the remedy?, shows this. The authors who see blocs and classes as the actors divide between exit (Dixon, Fitts, Webb) and a strong state (Krainer, with Norton and Hudson close by).

Weighing the evidence: what is the Iran war for, and is it working?

The charts compare the authors’ positions. This section asks which explanation fits the facts best. Facts come first: documents, public data and what authors report as having happened, not their interpretations. Each fact is marked as fitting an explanation, ruling against it, or not bearing on it. The best explanation is the one with the fewest solid facts against it. Facts that could be staged for appearances are flagged, and the ranking is shown both ways.

There are two questions, because a war can have a purpose and still fail. The first grid asks what the war is for. The second assumes a purposeful plan and asks whether it is working.

Hypotheses

The authors’ disagreements come down to a few competing explanations. Each card gives the claim, whose material fits it or cuts against it, what would test it, and where it stands. All come from the authors’ material; four (H1, H2, H9, H10) combine pieces from several authors into one model, and each card names whose pieces they are. The same list closes synthesis.md, and both are generated from the ledger.

So far the forecast record favours the readings built on written plans and on structure (H6, H5) over readings of Trump’s character; one collision between blocs, the diesel case, fits H1 and H4.

How to read this

  • A score is a position, not a grade. A 5 is not better than a 1.
  • Scores are whole numbers on a five-point scale, so a one-point gap is within my margin of judgement. Gaps of two or more are the ones to trust.
  • The margin and pair figures treat all thirteen axes as equally important. Change that and the ranking can move.
  • Left out because fewer than ten authors take a position, or because the answer is yes or no: crypto as exit or trap (four authors), depopulation (raised by Krainer, Fitts and Werner only), the Epstein files, a spiritual framing, whether Western workers gain or lose, Martenson's question of whether energy caps growth, and Hudson's of whether debts should be written down.
  • Berletic, Wilkerson, Diesen and Wolff have no score on digital control; Webb has none on China, the Iran war’s target, elections or who gains from the wars; Krainer none on the Iran war’s target; Martenson none on the multipolar bloc; Werner and Norton none on elections. I found no position in what I read.
  • Chomsky and K.J. Noh are cited in the synthesis as references and are not scored here: Chomsky has nothing on 2026, and Noh’s positions largely duplicate Norton’s, whose site he writes for.
