Teaching an AI to Doubt

An AI can read everything and quote anything, but left to itself it tends to accept the story it is told. This is what it took to make an AI think critically about a hard, contested subject.

I used an AI (Claude, from Anthropic) to help with a research project. The subject was the US empire: who controls it, how it works and why it goes to war. Sixteen independent thinkers, all of them critical of the US foreign-policy consensus, have written and spoken about it at length. The AI's job was to read them all and combine their views into one picture I could trust. The project itself, its method and its results, is described in a longer article. The main example the project tested its method on was the recent Iran war.

The project rests on one idea. A deception can fool a witness standing in one place, but it is very hard to stage something so that it looks right from every angle. Stage magic works this way: a trick looks right only from the seats the magician chooses. So instead of trusting any single expert, the project compared sixteen authors who look at US power from different vantage points: money, weapons, governments, hidden networks. Magicians also use misdirection, which fools every seat at once by drawing all eyes to one hand. Against that, the project followed what actually moved, such as money, oil and weapons, and used facts from people not arguing a theory. Where they agree despite looking from different places, the conclusion is more likely to be solid, although agreement among people chosen as critics of the consensus counts for less. Where they disagree, the project asks why. Often each author is describing a different part of the same thing, and both are right. Sometimes they start from different assumptions, and naming those assumptions helps. And sometimes one of them is simply wrong, which the facts can show.

The main lesson is simple. The AI did not lack intelligence; it lacked suspicion. Left to itself, the AI tended to accept the story as it was presented, and because it writes so fluently, the mistakes were hard to spot. What made the difference was building critical thinking into the process, as a set of rules the AI could not ignore. There were eight rules.

1. Look past the stated aims: ask who gains

The AI first judged the Iran war by its declared goals: stop Iran from getting a nuclear bomb, and change its government. Judged against those goals, the war looks like a failure so far, and some of the authors say so. John Mearsheimer, in an interview this year, called it the greatest strategic blunder in US history.

But the nuclear reason was weaker than it sounded, which leaves room for other aims. Inspectors had verified Iran's compliance with a nuclear deal until the US walked out of it in 2018. Iran then enriched uranium to 60%, far beyond what a power plant needs, and restricted inspectors' access, but US intelligence assessments up to 2025 still said Iran was not building a weapon.

During the war, the US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, described a different goal. Most of the world's oil has long been priced in US dollars, which many economists see as a pillar of the dollar's role as the world's main currency. Iran had been selling its oil to China without being paid in dollars. On 24 June 2026, Bessent told CNBC that "the Iranians will be invoicing in dollars" and that "Dollar dominance is essential." Asked whether this push to keep oil sold in dollars was a secret plan, the economist Michael Hudson, one of the sixteen, answered: "It's been very overt, very overt." Bessent's words show what the Treasury says it wants, not necessarily why the war was fought; the same caution applies to every official statement.

The rule: don't judge a war only by the aims its leaders announce. Ask who stands to gain, and look for evidence of that aim, such as what officials say in public. The outcome alone cannot settle it, because a war can fail its real aims too; rule 5 comes back to this.

2. Name the frame before answering

One of the first questions the project asked about each author was: "Is the US winning or losing?" That question quietly assumes that countries are the ones making the decisions. An assumption like that, built into the question itself, is a frame.

The frame decides what looks sensible. Glenn Diesen, a scholar who sees world politics as competition between states, calls Europe's policy towards Russia "completely irrational": judged as the policy of European countries acting for their own people, it makes no sense. Simon Dixon, a former banker, starts from a different frame: "America is not a country; it is a series of companies and a financial industrial complex."

When the question became "Who gains?", Diesen's puzzle became answerable. A policy can hurt Europe and still make money for others, such as American energy companies, whose share of Europe's imports of liquefied natural gas has kept rising. But "who gains" is itself a frame, and gain alone does not show who caused a policy; it only shows where to look. Most of the hard thinking in the project went into fixing the questions, not answering them.

3. Know each source's vantage point

Sixteen experts on one subject sound like sixteen opinions. But each author has a vantage point: the side of the subject they look from, usually set by their training and experience. A scholar of international relations looks at governments, a former banker at lending and debt, an investigative journalist at hidden connections between powerful people, and most of them look from more than one side. Sorted by vantage point, much of their disagreement turns out to be people describing different sides of the same thing.

In the chart, the authors are listed from top to bottom by one of their conclusions: whether power sits in governments (top, blue) or in networks above governments (bottom, orange). That order comes from what the authors conclude, not from their vantage points. The dots run, roughly, from top left to bottom right, but part of that pattern is built in: an author who studies hidden networks is likely to find power in them, and an author who studies states is likely to find it in states. The most telling columns are the ones least tied to either conclusion. Authors who worked inside institutions mostly conclude that power sits in governments (Fitts is the exception), and authors who look from energy or class are spread across the chart.

The weights in the chart were assigned by the project, so the chart illustrates the idea rather than proving it. It is consistent with vantage point shaping conclusions: part of the disagreement between the authors probably comes from where each one stands rather than from better or worse evidence. Each author is probably most reliable on the side they know best, and least reliable when judging the whole subject from that side. Mearsheimer argues persuasively that the war is failing its declared aims; whether the war made money for some Americans is a question his vantage point does not ask.

Why sixteen authors, not one

An AI that reads one author adopts that author's frame and extends it fluently. Several authors looking at the same problem contradict each other, and that supplies the doubt the AI lacks. This helps in three ways.

  • Agreement from different sides counts for more. Fourteen of the sixteen share a broad view that voters and elected officials are not the main decision-makers; who is, depends on the author: donors, banks, intelligence agencies or unelected officials. Because all sixteen were chosen from among critics of the official consensus, this agreement is weaker evidence than it looks. It counts most where authors reach it from very different starting points, such as credit, ownership, intelligence networks and diplomacy.
  • Hidden assumptions are easier to spot when authors with different vantage points describe the same thing. Every author makes assumptions they do not state. When you read only one author, those assumptions are hard to see. Rule 2 showed an example: Diesen's verdict that Europe's policy is irrational rests on his assumption that countries make the decisions, and that assumption only became visible next to Dixon's view of America as "a series of companies".
  • Each author brings a rival explanation. The project's evidence grid weighed five explanations of the Iran war: (1) control of the world's energy supplies, (2) Israel's own aims, (3) a move against China, (4) a blunder, and (5) profit for the arms, energy and banking industries. Each came from different specialists. The dollar aim in rule 1 came later, from officials' own words, and is tested separately. A single author would have offered one explanation and emphasised the facts that support it.

Authors from different fields still leave one gap, and the project was built to cover it. All sixteen authors are critics of the official consensus, and some share sources, so they can share blind spots. So the project added two checks from outside the group. The first is facts from people who are not arguing a theory, such as an energy analyst's count of how much oil actually left the Persian Gulf, or published estimates of how many missile interceptors the US had left. The second is tests that could prove an explanation wrong, such as the dated forecasts in rule 4; rule 5 shows how easy it is to get such a test wrong.

4. Know what can be checked, and when

A claim becomes a fact only after it has been checked against documents, data or reliable reporting. For events of the last few days there is often nothing to check against yet, so a claim about them is neither confirmed nor refuted: it is unchecked, and should be labelled that way. The AI tended to repeat such claims as if they were established.

Predictions are a different kind of claim. They cannot be checked today either, but they can be recorded with their date and checked when the time comes. Several of the people whose interviews I read, for example, predicted what the US will do in Iran after the November midterms. In a few months we will know who was right.

5. A failed plan is not proof that there was no plan

Some of the authors say the official reasons for the Iran war, a nuclear threat and a regime hated by its own people, were a cover. In their view, the real plan was to provoke Iran into striking oil facilities and shipping across the Persian Gulf. With Gulf oil cut off, Europe and Asia would have to buy American oil and gas and pay for it in dollars, and Iran itself would end up selling its oil for dollars too.

To check an explanation like this one, you need a result that could show it is wrong. The AI suggested one: if the war ended with Iran still selling its oil to China for Chinese yuan rather than dollars, that would show the war was not about the dollar.

But this suggestion is wrong: Iran still selling its oil for yuan would not show that the war was not about the dollar. The plan could have been real and simply have failed, because Iran proved much harder to defeat than expected. The economist Richard Wolff put it in one line: "the Iranians are something new", with drones and missiles "buried in places where the Americans can't get them." A failed plan is not proof that there was no plan.

So the question was split in two. Whether the plan existed is a question for evidence of intent: what officials said and wrote, before and during the war, such as the Treasury Secretary's words in rule 1. How the war ends shows only whether the plan worked: if Iran ends up selling its oil for dollars, it succeeded, and if Iran keeps selling for yuan, it failed.

Deception can also come in layers, and not everyone involved knows all of them. On one reading of the Iran war, three aims are stacked, each one a cover for the next. The first is the public aim: stop Iran from getting a nuclear weapon. The second is stated openly by officials, which makes it feel like the real answer: bring Iran's oil back into the dollar system. On this view, "regime change" is the wrong name for the goal. The goal is regime control: Iran's government does not need to be replaced, only brought into line, as long as it sells its oil for dollars. Venezuela may be an example: in January 2026 the US removed its president, Nicolás Maduro; his vice-president took over, the state was left standing, and its oil is now sold for dollars, as the US Treasury Secretary has said. The third is never stated, so it can only be inferred, and it is the hardest layer to show: cut oil production in the Persian Gulf, which squeezes China's energy supply and makes the world more dependent on American energy.

Not every player has the whole plan. On this reading, the people who know most are the planners: the intelligence services, military staff, think tanks and financial interests that design both the war and the story told about it. A president can sincerely believe in the first or second aim, while the actions he orders also serve the third. If so, honest insiders could describe the same war very differently because each was told a different part of it. Layers bring a risk. If the evidence shows that the dollar was not the real aim, a believer can always answer that the dollar was only a cover for the third aim. An explanation that can retreat to a deeper layer every time it is challenged can never be proved wrong. The answer is to look for evidence of intent for each aim separately: the nuclear aim against what officials said and knew about the threat at the time, the dollar aim against what officials say about how Iran is to be paid for its oil. Outcomes, such as Gulf oil output staying down while Europe and Asia buy more American energy, are consistent with the third aim but cannot prove it, because they would follow from almost any war in the Gulf.

The aims need not even be stacked. They can form a network, with the public aims meant for different audiences and each aim with its own chance of success, and with actions chosen because they pay off on several aims at once: a blockade of Iranian oil, for example, serves the dollar aim and the squeeze on Gulf oil at once. The same caution applies: a network of aims can fit almost any outcome, so each aim still needs its own evidence of intent.

6. Watch for accounts that bend facts to fit a theory

One widely followed interview, with a speaker who is not one of the sixteen, made four striking claims, and each one had a real core that had been stretched. Three examples: US officials meeting Canadian separatist leaders became US agents stirring up separatism; an agreement allowing two new US bases in Greenland became US control of Greenland; about $2 million that three donors spent to defeat a congressman became $30 million. Each was checked against published reporting, listed in the full synthesis. A strong theory pulls facts toward it. The defence is to check each claim against the record rather than to judge the person.

7. Check the AI's work with a program

Some of the worst mistakes in the project were made by the AI itself, not copied from the authors. They were hard to catch because they sounded right. Three examples:

  • The AI put its own summary of what an author said inside quotation marks, as if the author had said those exact words.
  • The AI turned a 2009 think-tank paper that only listed options into a plan that the US had supposedly been following ever since.
  • The AI credited me with ideas that the authors had published long before.

When the AI reread its own writing, it did not notice any of these mistakes. A simple program caught the first kind. The other two are errors of judgement rather than of wording, and I caught them on reading the draft. The program works like this. Every author's texts were saved in one folder per author, and the program checks every quotation in the full write-up against those saved texts, and every count such as "seven of the sixteen" against the list of names it summarises. More than 450 quotations have been checked. All of the 350-plus that are long enough to match word for word were found in the authors' own texts; the rest are terms of one or two words, too short to check. The program checks that a quote exists in the author's own words, not that it is used fairly, and quotes from outside the sixteen, such as officials, are checked by hand.

8. Don't defer to the human either

Critical thinking runs both ways. Early on, the AI rewrote the full write-up every time I made a remark, and I had to tell it: "don't take everything I say at face value." After that, the AI pushed back when I or a source went too far. When an analyst I was reading said that a bond yield rising by 19 basis points was a rise of 19%, the AI pointed out the mistake: 19 basis points is 0.19 of a percentage point. When I dismissed Iran's nuclear programme entirely, the AI pointed out that Iran really had enriched uranium to 60%, far beyond what a power plant needs, even if the case for war built on that fact was not convincing.

And sometimes the human knew better. Chris Martenson, who writes about energy and markets, often sounds unsure on camera when he talks about oil prices, and the AI read that as real uncertainty. I have followed his work for years, and in writing he is blunt: "the price of oil is so heavily manipulated at this point that veteran commodities traders and portfolio managers like Jeff Currie are throwing in the towel in exasperation." In one interview he does both in a single breath: he calls it "what I will politely call shenanigans", then says the markets "were clearly manipulated". People who depend on video platforms may speak more carefully on camera than on the page, because platforms can restrict videos that make strong accusations, so what looks like confusion on video may be caution. This is the reverse case of the rule: here the AI should have listened to someone who knew the source.

What it adds up to

Telling an AI once to think critically is not enough. The doubt has to be built into the process, as rules like the eight above, and checked by a program wherever it can be. Then keep a human in the loop who knows how the authors really talk, and an AI willing to say when that human has gone too far. In this project, the checks caught errors that fluent prose had hidden, because the human and the AI checked each other.
